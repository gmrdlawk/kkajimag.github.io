Two Days' Wages for a Single Concert: How High Will Ticket Prices Soar?

발행일: 2026년 10월 19일

Surging Ticket Prices

Going to a concert venue these days feels far from lightweight. Opening the ticketing page has naturally been followed by checking bank account balances and letting out a sigh. Back in 2017, all seats for a DAY6 concert at YES24 Live Hall were priced at 99,000 KRW. For their recent show, I paid 154,000 KRW. The price has jumped roughly 1.5-fold in about nine years. Granted, since the venue moved from a live hall to the far larger KSPO Dome, it is not an identical product. Still, it is undeniable that the weight felt by fans' wallets has drastically changed. This article does not claim that these ticket prices are inherently "unfair." Without transparent production costs and profit margins from talent agencies, there is no solid basis to judge fairness. Instead, it raises two fundamental questions: Are concert tickets appreciating faster than average incomes and consumer prices? And are fans given any visibility into how these prices are determined? According to the 2025 Concert Market Ticket Sales Analysis Report published by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism, Korean popular music (K-pop/K-music) ticket sales in 2025 surged by 29.0% year-on-year to reach 981.7 billion KRW. The total number of shows rose by 17.0%, while ticket bookings grew by 19.9%. As of 2025, the average price of a concert ticket surpassed 128,000 KRW. Alongside this rapid growth, the pricing structure itself has shifted. In 2022, HYBE introduced the "Sound Check" package to K-pop idol concerts. First unveiled at BTS's Permission to Dance on Stage – Seoul in March 2022, these sound-check tiers are priced 20% to 30% higher than general admission seats. Premium tiers like Sound Check and Meet & Greet are increasingly being layered on top of what used to be a single general admission option. Comparing the same artist across a four-year span illustrates this trend clearly. At BTS's show at Goyang Stadium this year, a Sound Check ticket cost 264,000 KRW, whereas a 2022 VIP seat (which included a sound check package) was 220,000 KRW. General admission (the lowest price tier) also rose from 165,000 KRW in 2022 to 198,000 KRW today—a 20% increase in four years. Even looking at a one-year gap for the same artist at the same venue reveals a steep curve. For BOYNEXTDOOR's Seoul concert held at KSPO Dome in July 2026, tickets were priced at 165,000 KRW for General, 220,000 KRW for Sound Check, and 253,000 KRW for Meet & Greet. In 2025, prices at the same venue were 154,000 KRW for General and 198,000 KRW for VIP (M&G/Sound Check). Unclear Causes & Structural Issues

Why are prices escalating? Korea Arts Management Service cites complex factors behind rising average ticket costs, including unfavorable exchange rates for visiting overseas acts, an increased frequency of large-venue shows, and a higher proportion of paid tickets. However, as this applies to the broader performing arts market, it fails to explain the specific mechanics of K-pop pricing. Industry experts point to a structural problem where other agencies simply follow suit whenever market leader HYBE raises its prices. While cost inflation and market leader price setting likely overlap, the lack of cost breakdown leaves fans in the dark about which factor weighs heavier.

Real Financial Burden: Labor & Hidden Fees

To put these numbers into context of human labor: under South Korea's 2026 minimum wage of 10,320 KRW per hour, an eight-hour workday yields 82,560 KRW before tax. At this rate, a 154,000 KRW ticket requires nearly two full workdays (1.9 days), while a 264,000 KRW BTS Sound Check seat demands over three full days of labor. Even factoring in mandatory weekly paid leave pay (approx. 99,000 KRW per day), they translate to roughly 1.6 and 2.7 working days, respectively. While minimum wage is a baseline and does not apply to every fan, many K-pop consumers are teenagers and young adults in their twenties without steady incomes. For those funding their fandom through allowances and part-time jobs, these conversions reflect real lived experience. Taking it further, BTS's World Tour PLAY&STAY package—a bundled deal offering tickets and luxury lodging at Paradise City Hotel—costs between 945,000 KRW and 1,390,000 KRW per person, demanding 11 to 17 full days of minimum-wage labor. Moreover, paying face value for a ticket is never the final price. Booking platform fees add another layer of expense. Starting November 16, NOL Ticket is raising its online booking fee from 2,000 KRW to 3,000 KRW—triple the rate prior to its 2023 adjustment. This makes NOL 1,000 KRW more expensive than competing platforms like Ticketlink and YES24 (both charging 2,000 KRW). This hike applies broadly across concerts and musicals, not just K-pop. While a 1,000 KRW increase might seem negligible relative to a 150,000 KRW ticket, booking multiple seats doubles the burden, not to mention additional ticket delivery fees. On top of these service charges, fans must often pay simply for the right to buy a ticket. The vast majority of major concerts restrict early access to official, paid fan club members. Annual membership fees—such as 25,000 KRW for BTS's ARMY or 45,000 KRW for DAY6's My Day—have effectively become a mandatory prerequisite for securing decent seats.

Scalping Dilemma

Those lucky enough to secure tickets at face value are the fortunate ones. Failing to do so can mean facing exorbitant secondary market prices. During a recent parliamentary audit, lawmakers highlighted an instance where a ticket for a popular concert, originally priced at 165,000 KRW, was listed on an overseas scalping platform for a staggering 6.2 million KRW (~$4,500 USD). This stark discrepancy signals that demand overwhelmingly outstrips supply even at official prices. Consequently, simply demanding "lower ticket prices" will not solve the issue; artificially suppressing face-value prices might only widen profit margins for scalpers. Enjoying live music and watching beloved artists perform is rapidly turning into a luxury. In this environment, fans are the first to suffer from burnout. As ticket prices continue to climb, it is only natural for consumers to demand high production value to match. Yet, some recent performances leave fans underwhelmed by declining vocal conditions, half-hearted choreography, uninspired stage lighting, confusing screen visual graphics, excessive VCR filler, and talk segments that overshadow actual music. Rather than taking fan loyalty for granted, the live music industry must urgently contemplate how to deliver genuinely high-quality, memorable performances at fair and justifiable prices.
